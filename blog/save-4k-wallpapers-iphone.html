Why wallpapers are the hardest image to save properly

Wallpaper sites are built to protect the full-resolution file. Right-click and long-press are usually disabled, the download button often opens another page first, and the picture on screen is frequently a resized copy of the original. Saving that copy is how you end up with a wallpaper that is sharp in Safari and soft everywhere else.

The second problem is that a single page rarely references one file. It references a thumbnail, a couple of intermediate sizes, sometimes a differently cropped mobile version, and sometimes an image painted on as a CSS background rather than a plain image tag. Which one you get depends entirely on where you tap.

What "HD" and "4K" actually mean on an iPhone

Two numbers matter, and only one of them is the one people quote.

  • Pixels. Modern iPhones have screens in the region of 1170–1320 pixels wide and 2530–2870 pixels tall. A wallpaper at or above that is sharp; anything below it gets scaled up by iOS and loses detail.
  • Aspect ratio. iPhone screens are close to 9:19.5 — a tall portrait rectangle. A 4K wallpaper is 3840 × 2160, which is landscape 16:9. It has four times the pixels of a full HD image and still makes a bad phone wallpaper, because setting it crops away more than half the picture.

So "4K" by itself tells you the file is big, not that it fits your phone. On a wallpaper site, look for the phone or portrait section first, then check the pixel dimensions. That combination — tall shape, enough pixels — is what makes a wallpaper look sharp.

Step 1: Copy the link to the wallpaper's page

Copy the URL of the page showing the wallpaper — not the image file, and not the site's download button. ImageGet reads the page itself, which is where all the different versions of the picture live.

Step 2: Paste it into ImageGet and tap Go

Open ImageGet, paste the link, and tap Go. ImageGet loads the page and collects every image it references: the preview, each size the site serves, and images set as page backgrounds. You get the whole set in one grid instead of one guessed file.

Step 3: Filter by resolution and keep the largest

This is the step that decides whether your wallpaper is sharp. Turn on Smart Filtering and set a minimum size — ImageGet drops the thumbnails and preview copies, leaving the full-resolution version of each image. If a page serves the same picture in several sizes, filtering is how you make sure the one you save is the big one.

Tap any image for a full-screen preview and pinch to zoom. At that zoom level the difference between a 400 px preview and a 3000 px original is obvious, so check before you commit.

Step 4: Save to your photo library

Tap Save All, and the images land in your photo library at the resolution the site published. Nothing is upscaled or recompressed on the way — if the site had a genuine 4K file, that is the file you get.

Your downloads are also kept in ImageGet's Local Folder, grouped by the date you saved them. If you are comparing a few wallpaper candidates, that is the place to go back to — the full-size versions stay there until you decide which one to set.

Step 5: Set it as your wallpaper in iOS

Open the saved image in Photos, tap the share button, and choose Use as Wallpaper — or go to Settings → Wallpaper → Add New Wallpaper → Photos. Two things are worth knowing here:

  • iOS zooms in. The lock screen scales and crops your image to fill the screen, so a picture that already matches the screen's aspect ratio loses the least.
  • Turn off Perspective Zoom if the wallpaper looks like it is sliding under the icons. It adds a parallax effect that shifts the image as you tilt the phone, which also crops the edges.

If the result still looks soft, the file was probably small to begin with — go back to the source page and look for the version with the largest pixel dimensions.

A quick wallpaper checklist

  • Portrait, not landscape — roughly 9:19.5 for a modern iPhone.
  • At least as many pixels as the screen: around 1170 × 2530 or more.
  • The important part of the picture near the middle, so cropping does not cut it off.
  • A file format your iPhone handles natively — JPEG, PNG, HEIC, or WebP.
